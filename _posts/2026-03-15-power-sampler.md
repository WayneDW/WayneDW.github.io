Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and Reinforcement Learning (RL) are two dominant paradigms for LLM post-training. While RL often generalizes better and preserves existing capabilities, SFT can suffer from catastrophic forgetting when learning from off-policy expert demonstrations. However, RL also has a fundamental limitation: it relies on the model's ability to discover correct trajectories through repeated sampling. What if the model cannot solve the problem in the first place?

A natural question is: Can we make SFT more on-policy without sacrificing the privileged information contained in expert demonstrations?

In Finetuning with Sampling: SFT Learns Better Than You Think, Karan, Chen, and Du (2026) propose Projection Sampling, an MCMC algorithm that transforms off-policy expert trajectories into more on-policy training data. Remarkably, standard SFT on these sampled trajectories can outperform strong RL and self-distillation baselines, without modifying the training objective.

Off-Policy vs. On-Policy Learning

Given a dataset of expert trajectories $$\mathcal D={(q_i,x_i)}_{i=1}^N$$, standard SFT minimizes the negative log-likelihood
$$-\mathbb E_{(q,x)\sim\mathcal D}\left[\log p_\theta(x\mid q)\right].$$

However, the expert data distribution $$\mathrm{p_{\mathrm{data}}}$$ can be significantly different from the base model distribution $$\mathrm{p_{\mathrm{base}}}$$. Consequently, SFT may require large parameter updates to imitate expert trajectories, potentially hurting generalization and prior capabilities.

On-policy RL alleviates this mismatch by training on trajectories generated by the model itself. However, for difficult problems, the probability of generating a correct solution may be extremely small.

For example, suppose the base model assigns only $$\mathrm{10^{-4}}$$ probability to generating a correct solution. Naive rejection sampling would require approximately $$\mathrm{10^4}$$ attempts to obtain a single correct trajectory.

SFT can access expert knowledge, while RL benefits from on-policy learning. Projection Sampling aims to combine both advantages by changing the training data distribution rather than the learning objective.

The remaining challenge is how to sample from $$\mathrm{p_C}$$ efficiently, especially when correct trajectories are extremely rare.

Metropolis-Hastings

Direct rejection sampling from $$\mathrm{p_C}$$ can be prohibitively expensive. Instead, Karan et al. propose a Metropolis-Hastings (MH) sampler initialized from an expert trajectory $$\mathrm{\tau\in C}$$.

Given a current trajectory $$\mathrm{x}$$, we first generate a candidate $$\mathrm{x'}$$ using an information-preserving proposal distribution $$\mathrm{\kappa_C(x'\mid x,\tau)}$$.

The proposed trajectory is accepted with probability

$$
\boxed{
\mathrm{
A(x',x)=
\min\left{
1,,
\frac{p(x')}{p(x)}
\frac{\kappa_C(x\mid x',\tau)}
{\kappa_C(x'\mid x,\tau)}
\right}.
}
}
$$

Here, the first ratio favors trajectories with higher likelihood under the base model, while the second ratio corrects for the proposal distribution.

The key idea is that the proposal can use privileged expert information, even though the target distribution is defined by the unconditioned base model restricted to correct trajectories.

To construct the proposal, the authors exploit the in-context learning capabilities of LLMs. Given a trajectory $$\mathrm{x_{0}}$$, they uniformly sample a random index $$\mathrm{m}$$, preserve the prefix $$\mathrm{x_{<m}}$$, and regenerate the remaining tokens:

$$
\mathrm{
x_t'\sim
p_{\mathrm{proposal}}(x_t\mid x_{<t}',\tau),
\qquad m\leq t\leq T.
}
$$

The proposal model receives the original question, the expert solution $$\mathrm{\tau}$$, and the partial trajectory. It is instructed to continue the reasoning in its own words while preserving the expert information and arriving at a correct solution.

Importantly, $$\mathrm{p_{\mathrm{proposal}}}$$ is not the target distribution. It is the base model conditioned on additional expert information, used to efficiently propose correct trajectories that the base model might otherwise struggle to discover.

To reduce the computational burden of sampling long sequences, the algorithm follows a block-wise procedure similar to Power Sampling: it gradually extends trajectories using a block size $$\mathrm{B}$$ and performs $$\mathrm{N_{\mathrm{MCMC}}}$$ MH steps at each stage.

Why Does Sampling Help SFT?

One attractive theoretical result is that the sampling distribution progressively approaches the base model distribution.

Let $$\mathrm{\pi_k}$$ denote the distribution after $$\mathrm{k}$$ MH steps, initialized from the off-policy expert data distribution. The authors show

$$
\boxed{
\mathrm{
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{k+1}|p)
\leq
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_k|p).
}
}
$$

The proof follows from the data processing inequality, using the fact that the MH transition kernel leaves $$\mathrm{p_C}$$ invariant and that, for distributions supported on $$\mathrm{C}$$,

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi|p_C)-\log P(C).
}
$$

Therefore, repeated MH updates produce training distributions that become progressively closer to the base model, while retaining the expert information.

This provides a theoretical explanation for why the resulting data may be easier to learn from: the model is no longer forced to imitate arbitrary off-policy expert trajectories, but instead learns from correct trajectories that are more compatible with its original distribution.

The guarantee applies to the idealized MH procedure with a valid information-preserving proposal. In practice, prompting an LLM to preserve correctness does not provide a strict mathematical guarantee, and finite-step sampling may still suffer from incomplete mixing.

Generalization and Catastrophic Forgetting

Empirically, the authors evaluate Projection Sampling on chemistry, mathematical reasoning, and medical question answering.

On chemistry using Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, sampling-based SFT achieves 66.0% accuracy, compared with 61.8% for both vanilla SFT and on-policy self-distillation (OPSD).

More importantly, the sampling-based model retains significantly more of its original capabilities:

The result is particularly interesting because the model learns a new scientific capability without the MMLU degradation observed under conventional SFT.

On the difficult MATH Levels 3–5 benchmark using Qwen2.5-3B, vanilla SFT actually decreases accuracy from 31.5% to 24.3%. By contrast, Sampling SFT reaches 49.5%, outperforming GRPO (45.7%) and UFT (47.0%).

Moreover, combining Sampling SFT with subsequent RL further improves performance to 54.5%, suggesting that sampling-based SFT and RL are complementary.

The authors also observe that increasing the number of MCMC steps decreases the distributional KL gap while improving downstream accuracy. A simple one-shot rewrite of expert trajectories is less effective than the full sampling procedure.

Beyond Distribution Sharpening

One natural concern is whether Projection Sampling simply reinforces capabilities already present in the base model, similar to distribution sharpening.

Interestingly, the authors find that this is not the case. On chemistry, the sampled-SFT model achieves better Pass@k performance than the base model and OPSD even at relatively large sampling budgets.

This suggests that the model has acquired additional capabilities rather than merely reallocating probability toward previously accessible correct solutions.

Unlike inference-time Power Sampling, which spends additional computation for each generated response, Projection Sampling incurs a one-time sampling cost during dataset construction. The resulting model can then be used with standard autoregressive decoding.

Discussion

The most interesting implication is that the gap between SFT and RL may not arise solely from their optimization objectives. The distribution of training trajectories can be equally important.

Projection Sampling offers a principled way to transform privileged off-policy expert data into more model-compatible supervision. Instead of modifying SFT with importance weighting, KL regularization, or RL objectives, it modifies the data distribution itself.

Nevertheless, several questions remain open. The theory assumes information-preserving proposals, but in-context expert guidance cannot guarantee correctness. Furthermore, the effectiveness of finite-step MCMC depends on proposal quality and mixing in an enormous sequence space. Projection Sampling also introduces additional data-generation computation that should be considered when comparing end-to-end training costs.

Despite these limitations, the work highlights a broader perspective: sampling is not only a tool for inference-time generation, but also a general-purpose operator for shaping training data.

Rather than asking only how to optimize a model on a fixed dataset, we can ask a complementary question: How should we sample the dataset so that the model can learn from it more effectively? -->

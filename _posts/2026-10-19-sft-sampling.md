Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) is widely used to teach LLMs new knowledge and reasoning capabilities. However, SFT often suffers from catastrophic forgetting, where learning new tasks degrades previously acquired capabilities. By contrast, Reinforcement Learning (RL), such as GRPO {% cite shao2024deepseekmath %}, often generalizes better but relies on the model's ability to discover correct solutions through exploration.

A natural question is: Can we combine the expert knowledge available to SFT with the on-policy advantages of RL?

In Finetuning with Sampling: SFT Learns Better Than You Think, {% cite karan2026reasoning %} propose a simple recipe: rather than directly training on off-policy expert demonstrations, sample new training trajectories that preserve expert knowledge while remaining close to the base model's distribution.

On-Policy vs. Off-Policy Data

Given an expert dataset $$\mathcal D$$, standard SFT minimizes


$$-\mathbb E_{(q,x)\sim\mathcal D}\left[\log p_\theta(x\mid q)\right].$$

However, expert trajectories may have very low likelihood under the base model. Learning from these off-policy demonstrations can require substantial changes to the model's distribution, potentially causing catastrophic forgetting.

On-policy RL avoids this mismatch by training on trajectories generated by the model itself. Nevertheless, when the probability of generating a correct solution is extremely low, RL struggles to discover useful training signals.

The key idea is to transform expert demonstrations into more on-policy training data before applying standard SFT.

Information Projection

Let $$\mathrm{p(x)}$$ denote the base model distribution over trajectories for a given question, and let $$\mathrm{C}$$ denote the set of correct trajectories that preserve the expert's information.

Ideally, we want to sample trajectories that satisfy the correctness constraints while remaining as close as possible to the base model:

$$\arg\min_{\pi:,\operatorname{supp}(\pi)\subseteq C}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi|p).$$

Interestingly, the optimal distribution has a simple closed-form solution:

$$
\frac{p(x)\mathbf{1}[x\in C]}{\sum_{x'}p(x')\mathbf{1}[x'\in C]}.
$$

In other words, the ideal training distribution is the base model distribution conditioned on generating correct, information-preserving solutions.

Consider a simple example where the base model assigns probabilities

$$\mathrm{p(a)=0.02,\quad p(b)=0.06,\quad p(c)=0.12,\quad p(d)=0.80,}$$

where $$\mathrm{a,b,c}$$ are correct trajectories and $$\mathrm{d}$$ is incorrect.

Conditioning on correctness gives

$$\mathrm{p_C(a)=0.1,\quad p_C(b)=0.3,\quad p_C(c)=0.6.}$$

Instead of directly imitating an arbitrary expert trajectory, the projected distribution favors correct trajectories that are already more natural for the base model.

Sampling Expert-Guided Trajectories

Directly sampling correct trajectories can be extremely inefficient when the model struggles with difficult problems. To address this issue, the authors introduce Projection Sampling, which uses expert demonstrations to guide the generation of new training trajectories.

The procedure is surprisingly simple:

Randomly select a position in the current trajectory.

Keep the prefix before the selected position.

Provide the expert solution as in-context information.

Let the base model continue generating from the preserved prefix, using its own reasoning style.

Accept or reject the new trajectory according to the sampling criterion, and repeat.

The third step is particularly important. Without expert guidance, the base model may struggle to complete a difficult reasoning problem correctly. By providing the expert solution as in-context information, the model can use knowledge it may not otherwise possess, while generating the continuation in its own words.

Importantly, the expert solution is used to guide the generation process, rather than directly determining the desired training distribution.

After several sampling iterations, the generated trajectories become more compatible with the base model while retaining the information contained in the expert demonstrations.

The resulting trajectories are then used for standard SFT, without changing the training objective.

Why Does Sampling Improve SFT?

The paper provides a theoretical explanation based on the distributional distance between training data and the base model.

Let $$\mathrm{\pi_k}$$ denote the distribution of sampled trajectories after $$\mathrm{k}$$ refinement steps. Under the idealized sampling assumptions, the authors show that

$$
\boxed{
\mathrm{
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{k+1}|p)
\leq
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_k|p).
}
}
$$

This means that the sampling distribution becomes progressively closer to the base model while preserving expert information.

Consequently, the model can learn new knowledge from training trajectories that are more consistent with its existing behavior, potentially reducing catastrophic forgetting.

Reasoning Performance and Catastrophic Forgetting

Empirically, Projection Sampling improves over standard SFT across several reasoning and knowledge-intensive tasks.

For chemistry using Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Sampling SFT achieves 66.0% accuracy, compared with 61.8% for vanilla SFT.

More importantly, standard SFT reduces MMLU accuracy from 68.7% to 58.6%, whereas Sampling SFT retains 69.2% MMLU accuracy.

### Chemistry and MMLU Performance

| Method | Chemistry | MMLU |
|:---|---:|---:|
| Base Model | 34.3% | 68.7% |
| SFT | 61.8% | 58.6% |
| OPSD | 61.8% | 65.1% |
| **Sampling SFT** | **66.0%** | **69.2%** |

### Mathematical Reasoning Performance

On challenging mathematical reasoning problems using Qwen2.5-3B, the improvement is even more striking:

| Method | Math Accuracy |
|:---|---:|
| Base Model | 31.5% |
| SFT | 24.3% |
| GRPO | 45.7% |
| UFT | 47.0% |
| Sampling SFT | 49.5% |

Interestingly, vanilla SFT performs worse than the base model, while Sampling SFT outperforms both GRPO and UFT.

Combining Sampling SFT with RL further improves accuracy to 54.5%, suggesting that the two approaches are complementary.

Discussion

The main takeaway is that the effectiveness of SFT depends not only on the correctness of expert demonstrations, but also on how compatible those demonstrations are with the model's existing distribution.

Rather than designing a more complicated training objective, Projection Sampling improves the distribution of training data before applying standard SFT.

The method also illustrates an interesting connection to inference-time sampling. While Power Sampling {% cite karan2026reasoning %} uses sampling to improve generation quality without additional training, Projection Sampling uses sampling to construct better training data.

In both cases, sampling provides a way to improve LLM behavior without necessarily changing the underlying model architecture.

One important limitation is that expert-guided generation does not strictly guarantee correctness, and the theoretical distributional guarantees rely on idealized sampling assumptions.

Nevertheless, the paper suggests a promising direction: instead of only asking how to train LLMs more effectively, we should also ask how to sample better data for training.
